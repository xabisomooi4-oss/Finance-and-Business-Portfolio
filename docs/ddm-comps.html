Chapter I

The Dividend Discount Model

The direct swap-in for a standard DCF when the business in question returns capital rather than reinvesting it.

Foundation

Time Value of Money, Revisited

A dollar received today is worth more than a dollar received in five years, because today's dollar can be invested and grow in the meantime. Every valuation method in this document — DDM, comps, and the two in the companion document — is ultimately a different way of applying this one idea.

Why this method, this company

Why DDM Fits BlackRock Better Than a DCF

A standard DCF discounts free cash flow — cash left over after a company funds its own operations and capital equipment. That framing assumes a business reinvesting heavily in physical or R&D capacity. Asset managers don't fit that mold: BlackRock's core "product" is managing other people's money, and it returns a large share of what it earns directly to shareholders as dividends rather than plowing it back into capex. DDM values the business on the actual mechanism by which shareholders get paid.

Why it mattersThis is a genuine methodology choice, not a simplification — real equity research on asset managers leans on DDM and comps specifically because of this structural difference.

The discount rate

Required Rate of Return (Cost of Equity)

DDM discounts at the cost of equity alone — not a blended WACC. The reason: dividends are a cash flow that reaches equity holders specifically, never debt holders, so there's no capital structure to blend. Cost of equity is typically estimated via CAPM: risk-free rate + (beta × equity risk premium).

Why it mattersUsing WACC here would be a real, substantive error, not a rounding difference — it discounts the wrong cash flow at the wrong rate for who actually receives it.

The forecast input

Dividend Growth Rate

Projected forward from BlackRock's own dividend history and payout policy. Asset managers with long, stable dividend-growth track records (as BlackRock has) make this a more defensible forecast input than, say, projecting revenue growth for an early-stage company.

Everything beyond the forecast window

Terminal Value (Gordon Growth Model)

The same mechanic as a DCF's terminal value, applied to the final projected dividend: one lump-sum estimate standing in for every dividend beyond the explicit forecast period, discounted back to today. As with any DCF-family model, this single number typically makes up the majority of the total value — the part most sensitive to the growth assumption.

Putting it together

The DDM, End to End

Project dividends per share for each forecast year, discount each back to today at the cost of equity, add a discounted terminal value, and sum. That sum is the model's estimate of intrinsic value per share.

Chapter II

Comparable Company Analysis

For a company like BlackRock, comps aren't just the sanity check — in practice, they're often the method analysts trust most.

A methodology note

Why Comps Carry Extra Weight Here

DDM depends on forecasting a growth rate and a discount rate decades into the future — both genuinely uncertain. Comps sidestep that by asking a more grounded question: what is the market actually paying right now for businesses that look like this one? For a mature, well-covered sector like traditional asset management, that market-based signal is often more trusted than a long-horizon forecast.

Choosing who counts

Selecting the Peer Set

State Street (STT), T. Rowe Price (TROW), Invesco (IVZ), and Franklin Resources (BEN) — chosen because they share BlackRock's core business model (fee income earned on assets under management), similar regulatory exposure, and comparable revenue drivers. A peer set is only as useful as the businesses are actually alike.

Why it mattersThe semiconductor peer set suggested earlier in this project (AMD, INTC, AVGO, QCOM) would have been meaningless here — comps only work when the businesses genuinely resemble each other.

The standard multiple

P/E Multiple

Price divided by earnings per share. Applying the peer group's average P/E to BlackRock's own EPS backs into an implied share price — the market's going rate for a dollar of this kind of earnings.

Capital-structure-neutral

EV/EBITDA Multiple

Enterprise value divided by EBITDA. Useful alongside P/E because it isn't distorted by differences in how much debt each peer carries — it values the operating business itself, independent of financing choices.

Putting it together

Comps, Side by Side

BlackRock has historically traded at a premium multiple to this peer set — largely attributed to its scale, its iShares/ETF franchise, and Aladdin, its technology platform (the subject of the companion SOTP document).

Reading the two methods together

Triangulating DDM and Comps

When DDM and comps land near each other, that's a meaningfully stronger signal than either alone. When they diverge, the gap itself is informative: DDM reflects a long-run assumption about payout policy and growth, while comps reflect what the market is willing to pay right now — a divergence often means the market is pricing in something (optimism, risk, a catalyst) that a steady-state dividend model doesn't capture.

Carrying forward
