I. Market Basics

The vocabulary everything else assumes you already have. Skim this even if some of it feels obvious.

Ownership

Share / Stock

A share is one unit of ownership in a company. Own a share of a company and you own a tiny, proportional sliver of that company itself — its cash, its factories, its future profits.

Why it mattersEvery number in these five projects — price, valuation, signal — is describing this one underlying thing: a claim on a real company.

Pricing

Market Price vs. Market Cap

Market price is what one share costs right now, set by the most recent trade. Market cap is that price multiplied by every share outstanding — the market's real-time price tag on the whole company.

Why it mattersYour Valuation Model exists purely to check whether market cap is too high or too low relative to what the company is actually worth.

Reporting

The Three Financial Statements

Every public company publishes three documents each quarter. The income statement shows profit over a period. The balance sheet shows what the company owns and owes at one moment. The cash flow statement shows actual cash moving in and out — the one that can't be dressed up with accounting choices.

Why it mattersYour DCF model is built entirely from numbers pulled off these three — mostly the cash flow statement.

Vocabulary

Revenue vs. Earnings vs. Free Cash Flow

Revenue is total sales. Earnings (net income) is what's left after every expense, including non-cash ones like depreciation. Free cash flow is the real cash left over after the business funds its own operations and equipment — the number an owner could actually pocket.

Why it mattersA DCF values a company on free cash flow specifically, never earnings — cash is what's real; earnings can be an accounting opinion.

Quick multiple

P/E Ratio

Price divided by earnings per share. It's shorthand for "how many years of current profit would it take to pay back this price" — the fastest way to compare whether one stock looks cheap or expensive next to another.

Why it mattersIt's the quick sanity check that supports — or contradicts — whatever your DCF says.

II. Valuation

The building blocks of a Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) model — the engine of your Valuation Model project.

Foundational idea

Time Value of Money

A dollar today is worth more than a dollar in five years, because today's dollar can be invested and grow in the meantime. This one idea is why you can't just add up a company's future cash flows — each future dollar has to be shrunk down, or "discounted," to what it's worth today.

Why it mattersThis is the entire mathematical premise the DCF model runs on. Everything else in this chapter is a consequence of it.

The discount rate

WACC (Weighted Average Cost of Capital)

The interest rate used to shrink future cash flows down to today's value, reflecting how risky those cash flows are and what an investor could earn elsewhere instead. WACC blends the cost of a company's debt and the cost of its equity into one rate.

Why it mattersThis is the single most sensitivity-heavy input in the whole model — nudge WACC by one percentage point and your fair-value output can swing 15–20%.

The long tail

Terminal Value

You can't forecast cash flows forever, so you project maybe 5–10 years explicitly, then estimate one lump-sum value standing in for "everything after that." In most real DCFs, this single number is over half the total valuation.

Why it mattersIt's the part of the model most vulnerable to an unrealistic assumption quietly wrecking the whole output — worth stress-testing.

Putting it together

The DCF, End to End

Forecast free cash flow for each future year, discount each year back to today at the WACC, add a discounted terminal value, and sum it all up. That sum is the company's estimated intrinsic value — compare it to market cap for a buy/sell read.

The reality check

Comparable Company Analysis (Comps)

Instead of forecasting the future, comps value a company by looking at what similar, already-public companies currently trade for (P/E, EV/EBITDA) and applying that multiple to your company's numbers.

Why it mattersIt's the check you run alongside the DCF so one bad assumption in your forecast doesn't go unnoticed — two independent methods agreeing is a much stronger claim than one.

III. Trading Signals

The technical toolkit your Agent draws entry/exit decisions from.

The base ingredient

Moving Average (SMA / EMA)

The average closing price over the last N days, recalculated daily so it "moves" with the stock. A simple moving average (SMA) weighs every day equally; an exponential moving average (EMA) weighs recent days more heavily. Either way, it smooths daily noise so the underlying trend is visible.

Why it mattersThis is the base ingredient nearly every other technical signal in your Agent is built from.

Momentum, binary version

Golden Cross / Death Cross

When a short-term moving average crosses above a long-term one, that's a "golden cross" — a classic bullish (buy) signal. The reverse, crossing below, is a "death cross" — bearish (sell).

Why it mattersThe simplest concrete entry/exit rule you can code in week one, and a fine baseline to improve on.

Overbought / oversold

RSI (Relative Strength Index)

A 0–100 oscillator measuring how fast and how far a price has moved recently. Above 70 is generally read as "overbought" (due for a pullback); below 30 as "oversold" (due for a bounce).

Why it mattersGives your Agent an exit signal that doesn't depend on waiting for a second moving average to cross.

Faster momentum read

MACD (Moving Average Convergence Divergence)

Tracks the gap between two EMAs (the "MACD line") against a smoothed average of that gap (the "signal line"); the histogram bars show the difference between the two. When the MACD line crosses the signal line, that's a momentum shift.

Why it mattersA more responsive cousin of the golden cross — popular because it reacts faster to trend changes.

Volatility-aware

Bollinger Bands

A band drawn two standard deviations above and below a moving average. Price hugging the upper band suggests strong momentum (or overbought); touching the lower band suggests the opposite. The bands widen and narrow with volatility itself.

Why it mattersGives your Agent a volatility-aware sense of "how far is too far," rather than a fixed threshold like RSI's 70/30.

The actual output

Entry / Exit Signal

The literal deliverable of your Agent: a decision, not just an indicator. In practice this means combining two or three of the tools above — e.g. "enter when a golden cross forms and RSI is below 70; exit when RSI crosses above 70 or a death cross forms" — so no single noisy indicator makes the call alone.

Why it mattersThis combination logic, not any one indicator, is what you'll actually be asked to explain and defend.

IV. Portfolio & Risk

How a collection of positions behaves together — the risk half of your Portfolio & Backtesting Model.

The goal

Diversification

Spreading capital across positions that don't all move together, so one holding's bad day doesn't sink the whole portfolio. The benefit is entirely a function of how correlated your holdings are — not how many you own.

Why it mattersThis is the actual justification for building a correlation matrix at all — it's not a separate exercise, it's how you measure whether diversification is real.

The measurement

Correlation

A number from −1 to +1 describing how two assets' returns move together: +1 means lockstep, −1 means exact opposites, 0 means unrelated.

Why it mattersA portfolio full of stocks highly correlated to each other isn't actually diversified, even if it "looks" like 20 different positions.

Risk, quantified

Volatility

The standard deviation of a stock's returns — a statistical measure of how much the price swings, in either direction, over time. Higher volatility means bigger, more frequent swings.

Why it mattersIt's the "risk" half of every risk-adjusted return metric, including the Sharpe ratio below.

Risk-adjusted return

Sharpe Ratio

Return earned above the risk-free rate, divided by volatility. It answers "how much return am I getting per unit of risk taken" — the standard way to compare two strategies that had different returns and different risk.

Why it mattersThis is likely the single number a reader uses to judge whether your backtested strategy is actually good, or just lucky.

Survivability

Max Drawdown

The largest peak-to-trough decline your portfolio experiences during the period tested, as a percentage. A strategy can have a great average return and still be unsurvivable if its max drawdown is 60%.

Why it mattersThis is the "could a real investor have actually stomached this" check on your backtest — see the equity-curve diagram in Chapter V.

Market exposure

Beta

How much a stock or portfolio moves relative to the overall market (usually the S&P 500). A beta of 1.5 means the position tends to swing 50% more than the market in either direction.

Why it mattersTells you whether your portfolio's risk is coming from genuine stock-picking or just from carrying more market exposure than average.

V. Backtesting

Turning a strategy into evidence — the other half of the Portfolio & Backtesting Model.

The core idea

Backtest

Running your strategy's rules against historical data to see what would have happened, as if you'd traded it for real. It's the only way to gather evidence for a strategy before risking real money on it.

Why it mattersThis chart is the headline deliverable of your Portfolio & Backtesting Model — it's the single image that proves the whole project worked.

The classic trap

Lookahead Bias

The single most common way a backtest lies to you: accidentally letting the strategy "see" information it wouldn't have had yet — for example, using a day's closing price to decide whether to buy earlier that same day.

Why it mattersUndetected, your results will look great and be completely fake. Explicitly stating you checked for this is a credibility signal in your write-up.

Guarding against overfitting

Walk-Forward Testing

Instead of testing a strategy once on the full historical dataset, you repeatedly tune it on one time window and test it on the very next, unseen window, then roll forward.

Why it mattersProtects against a strategy that was accidentally over-fit to one lucky historical period rather than genuinely predictive.

The unglamorous detail

Transaction Costs & Slippage

The commission you pay and the small, unfavorable gap between the price you saw and the price you actually got filled at. A strategy that looks profitable before costs can easily be a loser after them.

Why it mattersIncluding this detail signals you understand backtesting is not just a math exercise — it's the difference between a toy model and a credible one.

VI. The Agent

What actually makes item 1 an "agent" rather than a script.

The brain

LLM (Large Language Model)

The AI model (like Claude) that can read text, reason over it, and generate a response — including deciding what to do next, not just what to say.

Why it mattersIt's what turns your rule-based signals into something that can also read a news headline and explain its reasoning in plain English.

How it acts

Tool Calling

The mechanism that lets an LLM actually do things instead of just talking. You give it a defined set of functions — e.g. get_stock_price(), get_moving_average() — describe what each does, and the model decides when to call one and with what arguments.

Why it mattersThis is exactly how your Agent would pull live data instead of you hardcoding a fixed dataset.

Why it's an "agent"

The Agent Loop

The repeating cycle an agent runs: observe (pull current data) → reason (the LLM decides what it means) → act (call a tool for more data, or produce a decision) → observe the result → repeat, until it reaches a final answer.

Why it mattersThis is why item 1 on your list is called an Agent and not a screener — be ready to explain this loop specifically.

What the model can see

Context / Prompt

The full set of information the LLM sees before responding: your instructions, the conversation so far, and any data the tools returned. The model has no memory beyond what's inside this window.

Why it mattersYour Agent's quality lives or dies on what data you feed into its context — vague or missing data produces a vague or wrong signal, same as it would for a human analyst.
