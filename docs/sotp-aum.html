Chapter I

Sum-of-the-Parts (SOTP)

A single blended multiple assumes a single business. BlackRock has three genuinely different ones under one ticker.

The core idea

What SOTP Is

Instead of applying one multiple to a company's total earnings, SOTP values each distinct business segment separately — using the multiple appropriate to that segment's own risk and growth profile — then sums the pieces. It's more work than a single blended multiple, but it's more honest whenever a company is genuinely several businesses wearing one ticker.

Why BlackRock specifically

Three Businesses, One Ticker

BlackRock combines active management (traditional fund management, fees tied to performance and scale), iShares/ETFs (passive, high-volume, lower fee rate but enormous asset base), and Aladdin (a technology and risk-management platform other financial institutions pay to license). A single "asset manager" multiple applied to the whole company either overpays for the slow-growth pieces or underpays for the fast-growing one.

Why it mattersThis is precisely the gap DDM and a single comps multiple both leave open — neither method looks inside the company at what's actually driving its growth.

Segment one

Active Management

Traditional, actively-managed funds where returns depend on manager skill and market performance. Valued the way a conventional asset manager is valued — the same logic behind the peer set in the companion document (STT, TROW, IVZ, BEN).

Segment two

iShares & Passive / ETFs

Lower fee rates per dollar managed, but an enormous and still-growing asset base. Valued more on scale and growth trajectory than on manager skill — a volume business, not a stock-picking one.

Segment three

Aladdin (Technology Platform)

Aladdin licenses BlackRock's risk-management and portfolio software to other institutions, generating recurring, subscription-like revenue. This behaves like a software (SaaS) business, not an asset manager — and software businesses command materially higher revenue multiples than fee-based money management.

Why it mattersThis is the single biggest reason BlackRock trades at a premium to its comps peer set: the market is pricing in a technology business that the peer group simply doesn't have.

Putting it together

The SOTP Bridge

Value each segment on its own appropriate multiple, then sum the three to arrive at total implied equity value — a materially different, more granular answer than one blended multiple could produce.

Chapter II

AUM-Based Valuation

The one number the entire business is a leveraged bet on.

The core metric

What AUM Is

Assets Under Management — the total market value of the money BlackRock manages on behalf of clients. Critically, this is not BlackRock's own money or revenue; it's the base its management fees are calculated against.

Why it drives everything

The AUM → Revenue → Earnings Chain

Average fee rate × AUM = revenue. Because fee rates move slowly and are largely set by competitive market forces, AUM growth is, to a first approximation, revenue growth. The entire business is essentially a leveraged bet on this one number continuing to climb.

The industry-specific check

Price-to-AUM as a Sanity Check

Some analysts use price relative to AUM as a rough, industry-specific gut-check against DDM, comps, and SOTP — not a primary method, but a fast way to ask "does this valuation make sense relative to the sheer scale of money being managed."

The honest risk in the whole model

Fee Compression

Average fee rates across the asset management industry have been declining for years, driven largely by the shift toward low-cost passive investing. This means AUM growth alone doesn't guarantee revenue growth — if fee rates keep falling faster than AUM grows, revenue can stagnate even as the "assets managed" headline number keeps climbing.

Why it mattersThis is the single most important caveat to raise if anyone asks you to defend this valuation — a rising AUM number, on its own, is not the same thing as a company getting more valuable.

Putting it together

The AUM Chain

AUM, multiplied by the fee rate it earns, becomes revenue; revenue, multiplied by margin and an appropriate multiple, becomes value. Fee compression is the pressure working against this chain at every step.

Reading all four methods together

DDM gives a long-run, payout-based estimate. Comps gives a market-based reality check. SOTP accounts for Aladdin specifically. AUM-based valuation stress-tests the whole thing against the one metric the business actually runs on. No single method is "the" answer — the strength of a real valuation is in how these four triangulate, and in being able to explain why they might disagree.
